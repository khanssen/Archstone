The Federal Compliance Rulemap - Where FISMA, FedRAMP, and CUI rules actually stand in 2026

Buckle up, buttercup, because things are about to get crazy. There are 3 hot topics affecting Federal regulations right now: a proposed FAR CUI rule, FedRAMP CR26 / 20x restructure, and the CMMC third-party verification suspension. This infographic is designed to help you understand what Federal program guidance relies on what other guidance, what is affected by the current and proposed changes, and what will need to be modified to incorporate consistency. This interactive map traces applicable guidance for FISMA, FedRAMP, CMMC, and CUI regulatory initiatives: what they require, which version of the NIST guidance it points to, and where these rules have stopped lining up. You can click through the rules to learn Where one now cites something that no longer exists and where a codified regulation and a press release flatly contradict each other, and nobody with the authority to reconcile them has said which one wins.
Why this map exists
Federal data protection is a stack, not a list. FISMA sits at the top: every agency owns a statutory duty to secure its systems against NIST control baselines. FedRAMP is the collaborative mechanism that lets an agency satisfy part of that duty by reusing a cloud authorization. CUI reaches contractors through two enforcement lanes on top of that stack: the defense lane (DFARS clauses & CMMC) and the government-wide lane (the proposed FAR CUI rule); and both collide with the cloud authorization regimes (FedRAMP, the DISA SRG) wherever CUI lives in cloud services.
Three of the load-bearing programs are now running on broken process. CMMC's third-party verification was suspended by announcement on July 13, 2026. FedRAMP's June 26 restructure (20x) replaced control-by-control assessment with automated, largely provider-supplied checks and deprecated the shared “Moderate” baseline the rest of the government still needs before it can use cloud services. That baseline (FISMA) obligates every agency to secure its systems against NIST control baselines, and FedRAMP exists to coordinate Federal agencies' receipt of security assessment enabling all of them to rely on one independent assessment instead of conducting their own. When a 20x package speaks in Key Security Indicators instead of 800-53 controls, the agency inheriting loses its ability to translate risk in the language it's required to speak. In 20x, the assurance behind the package is diminished: 20x Class A is self-attested with independent assessment optional, and even Class B and C rely on automated, provider-supplied evidence rather than an assessor actually testing the controls. The proposed FAR CUI rule meant to standardize protection government-wide anchors to a Defense rulemaking that was suspended twenty days after the rule published - its public comment window closes July 23, 2026, the one place that fracture goes on the record before the rule is finalized.
